Leapstone

Chinese checkers by the numbers

The star board of Chinese checkers has 121 spots, and each player starts with 10 pieces. In Leapstone, one move can never have more than 36 jumps. A player alone on the board, with no opponents, needs 27 moves to cross it. Below you can see how each number is counted, and what it does and does not tell you about a real game.

Spots and pieces

Counted on the two boards of Leapstone, in the starting position of a game.

  • The star board has 121 spots. Each player starts with 10 pieces. So a game for two players has 20 pieces on the board, a game for three has 30, for four 40 and for six 60.
  • The square Halma board has 256 spots. In a game for two players, each player starts with 19 pieces, 38 in all. In a game for four, each starts with 13 pieces, 52 in all.

The longest chain of jumps: 36

On the star board, one piece can make 36 jumps in a single move, and under the rules of Leapstone no move can have more. To reach that number, the other pieces are placed freely: one on every spot the piece jumps over.

How it is counted. A jump always lands two spots away in a straight line. So a piece that keeps jumping can only reach some of the spots: the board splits into four such groups, and the largest has 37 spots. In Leapstone, a piece may not land on the same spot twice in one move, or on the spot where it started, so a chain lands at most once on each of the other spots in its group. The program searched that group and found a path that visits all 37 spots, so no chain can be longer: 36 jumps. A test then plays that move with the rules of Leapstone.

Not a real game. This chain needs 36 other pieces, each on exactly the right spot. A game for two or three players has only 20 or 30 pieces, which is too few. A game for four or six players has 40 or 60, which is enough. But in a real game nobody places pieces freely, so do not expect this chain at the table. It shows what the board allows, not what happens in play.

The shortest crossing: 27 moves

One player alone on the star board, with no opponents, needs 27 moves to bring all 10 pieces from one point to the point across, and cannot do it in fewer. A whole chain of jumps counts as one move.

How it is counted. A test plays a crossing of 27 moves with the rules of Leapstone, with no other pieces on the board. To show that 26 moves or fewer cannot work, a search follows the method of G. I. Bell (Integers 9, 2009). Every move can be played backwards, and turning the board half a turn swaps the start and the goal. So if a crossing of 26 moves or fewer existed, some position halfway would be at most 13 moves from the start, and so would the same position turned half a turn. The search went through every position within 13 moves of the start, counted a position and its mirror image once, left out the ones that provably cannot get far enough in time, and found no such pair. It ran for 827 seconds on a machine with 8 cores, and its largest step held 28.8 million positions in 2.65 GB of memory. The number was worked out again for Leapstone's own board.

Not a lower limit for a real game. In a real game, the pieces of other players are on the board too, and your pieces can jump over them. So 27 is not the least a real game can take: it is only proven for one player alone.
